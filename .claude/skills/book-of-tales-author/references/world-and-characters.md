# World, themes and characters

What the published book's Britain is like, who lives in it, and how each character card and feature becomes a person on the page.

## The themes

These run under nearly every encounter. A good encounter touches at least one.

1. **Chivalry tested by temptation.** Almost every scene offers a nobler road and a more profitable one. The book does not preach. It makes the wicked road pay (see story-tokens-and-renown.md).
2. **Justice against mercy.** Knights are constantly asked to judge: village trials, accused squires, thieves with hungry children. There are rarely clean answers.
3. **Ordinary people's troubles.** Farmers, orphans, lepers, goose-girls, jesters and dwarves bring most of the problems. Kings and queens are a minority of the cast.
4. **Faith and its price.** The Grail, relics, hermits, penance and miracles. Holiness is powerful and costly, and pride before it is punished.
5. **The fay's bargains.** The Otherworld is beautiful, capricious and dangerous. Gifts come with conditions. Rudeness is punished out of all proportion.
6. **Love against duty.** Illicit loves, arranged marriages, jealous kin. Romance is rewarded, and it also causes the book's worst disasters.
7. **Loyalty and betrayal.** Serve Arthur or his enemies. The book lets you choose either, and remembers.
8. **An ideal in decline.** Across the three ages, Camelot rises, reaches for the divine, and falls. The same forest looks different in each.
9. **Human folly.** Boasting knights, pedantic seneschals, lovesick giants, bad poets. The book is often funny.

## The three ages

Each age changes the mood of the whole book. Milieu passages are written three times, once per age, and character encounters can check the age.

### Age 1: the Golden Age of Camelot

- **Mood:** bright, adventurous, political. Sunlight, tournaments, new alliances.
- **Events in the air:** Arthur's early reign and the kings who rebel against it (King Lot of Orkney, and invaders from over the sea); the Round Table's forming; Merlin active at court; Excalibur; the Questing Beast; young loves and old feuds beginning.
- **Grail** material appears only as prophecy, relics and ancient guilt, such as a king struck blind long ago for looking into it.

### Age 2: the Quest of the Holy Grail

- **Mood:** solemn, visionary, penitential. Processions, grottoes, saints' last words.
- **The opening:** at a Pentecost feast the Grail appears veiled, passes through the hall and vanishes. The land becomes a Wasteland, and every knight feels its call.
- **Events in the air:** the Grail Castle (Corbenic) and its wounded king; hermits and anchoresses who read dreams; miraculous ships; tests of purity; sin revealed.
- Milieus take on religious colour. A cave that hid treasure in Age 1 might hold a relic, a vision or a penitent in Age 2.

### Age 3: the Final Wars of Britain

- **Mood:** grim, elegiac, loyalties strained. Dim light, burnt fields, ravens.
- **The opening:** an ageing Arthur summons his knights. Mordred has accused the queen of treason and invited the Saxons in. The great houses of Orkney and Benwick are at each other's throats.
- **Events in the air:** Arthur against Mordred; Saxon fleets; sieges; the passing of the old order, and Avalon waiting.
- Milieus often have the war intrude into the background: a battle on the next hill, a wounded creature needing help while your allies fight. This forces a choice between the scene in front of you and the war.

### Writing one scene across three ages

About half of the book's milieu slots reuse a shared opening frame in all three ages, then change what the knight finds. The light is the tell:

- *golden sunlight* (Age 1) → *dappled sunlight* (Age 2) → *dim sunlight* (Age 3);
- a curious creature → a creature that has been *waiting* for you → a wounded creature, with the war nearby.

It is a strong technique. Use it for about half your milieus, and write the other half fresh so the book doesn't feel mechanical.

## The recurring cast

These appear across many encounters in the published book. All are figures of legend and free to use, but write them in your own words.

**Off limits: the eight player characters.** Sir Lancelot, Sir Palomides, Sir Gawain, Sir Percival, Sir Galahad, Sir Tristan, Dame Enid and Dame Bradamante are the playable knights and dames. The published book names none of them once in about 470,000 words, because the reader might be any of them. Characters can still refer to the great houses (Orkney, Benwick) without naming them. Their kin and loves are usable, sparingly: Erec, Isolde, King Mark and Sir Safir all appear. Never have one of them speak *about* the missing hero by name.

| Character | Weight | How the book uses them |
|---|---|---|
| **King Arthur** | very high | Mostly offstage: the king whose side you are on or against. When present he is warm, decisive, ageing across the ages. |
| **Merlin** | high | An adviser, a riddler, a test-giver, often met in the wild. Knowing, dry, a little amused. |
| **Mordred** | high | Arthur's illegitimate son. A presence in all ages, rising to the villain of Age 3. |
| **Sir Kay** | high | Arthur's seneschal and foster-brother. Blunt, tart-tongued, bullies the court fool, but is loyal. |
| **Sir Gareth, Sir Gaheris, Sir Agravain** | high | The brothers of Orkney. Gareth is the gentle, well-liked one. Gaheris and Agravain are hot-headed and jealous of family honour. |
| **King Lot and Queen Morgause of Orkney** | high | A rebel king in Age 1, and a queen who is magnetic, worldly and scandalous. |
| **Morgan le Fay** | high | Sorceress queen, Arthur's half-sister. Schemes, captures, tests, and occasionally helps. |
| **Nimue, Vivian, the Lady of the Lake** | medium | The lake's enchantresses: gracious, dangerous, bound up with Merlin and Excalibur. |
| **Sir Bors and Sir Lionel** | medium | Brothers of Benwick. Bors is devout and true. Lionel is proud and easily provoked. |
| **Oberon, Titania and Puck** | medium | A faerie court in the Shakespearean mould. Oberon is regal and ironic, Titania wilful, and Puck a mischief-maker who sometimes needs rescuing. |
| **The Green Knight** | medium | The Green Chapel's lord. His terms are strict, his word is kept, his humour is grim. |
| **King Pellinore** | medium | An aged, eccentric king forever chasing the Questing Beast. Comic and oddly touching. |
| **Sir Lamorak** | medium | A great knight, lover of Queen Morgause, a magnet for the Orkney brothers' rage. |
| **King Mark and Queen Isolde of Cornwall** | medium | A jealous king and his unhappy queen, usually mid-flight or mid-pursuit. |
| **Sir Dinadan** | medium | A prankster knight who mocks chivalry's pretensions. |
| **Sir Dagonet** | low | Arthur's fool, tormented by Kay. |
| **Sir Bedivere, Sir Lucan, Sir Griflet, Sir Ector de Maris** | low | Loyal knights of the court, often companions in a scene, prominent in Age 3. |
| **King Pellam, the Fisher King** | low | The wounded Grail king of Corbenic. |
| **King Uther** | low | Arthur's father, usually a restless ghost. |
| **Taliesin** | low | A bard whose stories are true and whose poetry can be taught. |
| **Herne the Hunter** | low | An antlered wild huntsman, dangerous and ancient. |
| **Queen Guinevere** | low | Present mostly through others' talk: summoned, accused, defended. |

Beyond these, the published book names about 200 people, **most of them invented for a single encounter**. That is the book's texture. Yours might be a ferryman who takes secrets instead of coin, a relic-seller whose relics are pig bones, a blacksmith's daughter forging her own armour, or a dwarf herald with a grievance. **Invent your own. Do not reuse the published book's invented characters.**

## Character cards: what each one becomes

A character card is a *type*, not a person. Each of the 11 feature encounters for that card invents a different figure of that type. The book likes to subvert the type: giants are often gentle, hags are often wronged, and the pious hermit may be a fraud.

| Card | Who the book makes of it | Tone |
|---|---|---|
| **King** | named kings of legend: rebel kings, eccentric kings, cursed and ancient kings, a king convinced he is already dead | politics, loyalty, strange dignity |
| **Queen** | queens of legend (Morgause, Isolde, an Irish warrior-queen), a queen in disguise as an abbess or a beggar, sorceress-queens | love, scandal, power, piety |
| **Knight** | knights of the Round Table and their rivals, a foreign knight on pilgrimage, a knight in cursed armour | honour, challenge, errantry |
| **Squire** | squires of famous knights and ordinary youths: a squire forging his master's letters, secret keepers, the accused, one young woman in disguise | youth, loyalty, awkward ambition, often comic |
| **Maiden** | legend's maidens and ordinary spirited girls: a novice fled from her convent, a girl sworn to find her father's killer | agency, danger, courtship, faith |
| **Hunter** | huntresses, trackers, ghosts, fay huntsmen, a trapper who has caught something that talks | the wild, pursuit, the line between hunter and hunted |
| **Hermit** | anchoresses, lepers, mad monks, frauds, holy men who know your name | counsel, penance, revelation |
| **Rogue** | braggarts, thieves on trial, grave-robbers, charlatans, outlaws | comic greed, moral grey, small justice |
| **Fool** | jesters and motley wanderers: riddlers, grieving fools, fools caught in plots | wit, pathos, sharp truth spoken lightly |
| **Giant** | giants and giantesses: lovesick, grieving, oppressive, wounded, or simply in the way | scale, pathos, surprise |
| **Dwarf** | dwarves as heralds, nuns, knights, mercenaries, accused men | dignity against prejudice, faith, trickery |
| **Faerie** | Celtic fay: hags, changelings, banshee-like mourners, a leprechaun, the Light and Dark Fay | capricious bargains, curses, transformation |

## Features: the dramatic mode

The feature is the encounter's *situation*, and it inflects any character card. From the published book's 132 character encounters:

| Feature (offset) | The situation it creates |
|---|---|
| **Wandering** (1) | met on the road, away from home; a figure in motion with a request |
| **Sorrowful** (2) | grief: a lost love, a dead master, a ghost, a lament, a curse on a family |
| **Blessed** (3) | holiness: a vision, a relic, a saint, a holy test, sometimes a false blessing |
| **Enchanted** (4) | magic at work: a spell, a poet's glamour, a transformation, a fay mask |
| **Amorous** (5) | love: a triangle, a wedding, a clumsy suitor, a seduction, a jealous kinsman |
| **Mad** (7) | madness or delusion: bewitched, possessed, obsessed, or mad with grief |
| **Vengeful** (10) | revenge: a wronged party wants blood, and the knight is asked to help or stop it |
| **Wise** (12) | counsel or test: a teacher, a war council, an oracle, a riddle |
| **Cunning** (13) | schemes: a prank, a heist, a disguise, a trap, a bargain with a catch |
| **Lost** (15) | something missing: a lost traveller, lost honour, lost treasure, a lost child or beast |
| **Desperate** (20) | the extreme: trials and executions, the dying, a curse only you can lift |

## Places

Use real and legendary geography, lightly: Camelot, Logres, Cornwall and Tintagel, Orkney, Benwick (in Brittany), Cameliard, Gore, Normandy, Ireland, Wales, Corbenic, Avalon, Kells, Stonehenge, the Callanish Stones, the Green Chapel. The components file lists the twelve locations with Places of Power. Their visit passages (25xx) are the grandest scenes in the book: write them as destinations.

Terrain shapes milieus: **City** (courts, markets, plague, riots, feasts), **Forest** (outlaws, fay, hunts, ruins), **Mountain** (nests, temples, chasms, strongholds carved in rock), **Plains** (battles, tournaments, old wells, heaths), **Swamp** (bogs, disease, witches, will-o'-wisps), **Sea** (storms, islands not on any map, Viking and Saxon ships, kelpies).

## Creatures

A medieval bestiary plus British and Irish folklore: dragons (sometimes talking, sometimes enchanted), giants and ogres, unicorns, griffins, kelpies, manticores, basilisks, the catoblepas, the Questing Beast, wolves and great boars, brownies turned boggart, changelings, leprechauns, demons and restless dead. Treat monsters as characters: many can be reasoned with, pitied or tricked, not only killed.

## Sources to draw on

The published book mines public-domain legend widely. You should too, telling the stories in your own words:

- Malory's *Le Morte d'Arthur*: most of the named knights, kings and feuds
- The *Mabinogion*: Culhwch and Olwen, Owain and his lion, Taliesin
- Chrétien de Troyes, and *Sir Gawain and the Green Knight* (the Green Chapel's terms)
- *The Wedding of Sir Gawain and Dame Ragnelle* (the loathly lady)
- Irish myth: Queen Maeve and the Cattle Raid, the fay
- *A Midsummer Night's Dream*: Oberon, Titania and Puck
- Saints' lives (St Winifred and her well), Grail romances (Joseph of Arimathea, Solomon's ship)
- British folklore: Herne, kelpies, boggarts, the wild hunt

When you use a legend, **enter it at an unusual angle.** Meet the story's minor figure, or arrive halfway through, or give the knight a choice the legend never offered. The published book's best encounters take a famous tale and ask the player what *they* would have done.
