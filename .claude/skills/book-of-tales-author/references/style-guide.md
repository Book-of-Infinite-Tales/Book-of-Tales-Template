# Style guide

How a Book of Tales passage sounds, and how to write one. Every example in this file was written for this guide. None is taken from the published book.

## The voice in one paragraph

The book reads like a good storyteller running a tabletop game: plain, clear, modern English that moves briskly from situation to choice, with a light old-fashioned flavour in how people speak. It is concrete. Someone is standing somewhere, wants something and says so. It is often funny: dry, situational humour from fools, boastful knights and stubborn peasants. It is also willing to be dark: a clumsy word at the wrong moment can get someone killed. It never lectures, and it never stops to explain what the scene means. The meaning is in what people do.

## The numbers

Measured across the published book. Use them as targets, not limits.

| Measure | Typical | Range (10th–90th percentile) |
|---|---|---|
| Response passage body (before the choices) | 150 words | 80–300 |
| Resolution lead-in (before the skills are offered) | 35 words | 0–110 |
| One outcome body (success *or* failure) | 110 words | 40–220 |
| Result passage (no roll) | 80 words | 10–340 |
| Walk-away result | 1–3 sentences | — |
| Narration sentence | 16 words | 8–30 |
| Share of text that is dialogue | about 20% | — |

Short is normal. A response of 80 words is not underwritten if the situation is clear.

## Narration

**Second person, present tense, always.** "You come upon a mill", not "You came upon". Past tense only inside a character's speech or for backstory.

**Open on the road, then get to the point in one sentence.** About one response in five opens with "As you…". Most of the rest open with a travel verb: *You come upon*, *You arrive at*, *You hear*, *During your travels*, *Not far ahead*. By the second sentence the reader should know what is wrong or strange.

- *As you cross a frozen mere at first light, the ice groans beneath your horse and a voice below it asks you to stop.*
- *You come upon a crossroads where a gibbet stands empty and a boy sits beneath it, sharpening a very large knife.*
- *During your travels, you are overtaken by a bishop riding backward on a mule.*

**Concrete detail, one or two per character.** A dented helm tied on with twine. A tabard three sizes too big. Hands stained blue to the wrist. Choose details that suggest a story, not ones that only set a mood.

**The knight may react, briefly.** "You are curious." "You suspect the stranger has told you only half of it." "Shaken, you ride on in silence." One clause of feeling, then back to events. Do not analyse the knight's inner life.

**Verbs do the work.** People *stride*, *squelch*, *bellow*, *snatch*, *kneel*, *hurl*, *wheeze*. Use few adverbs and fewer abstract nouns.

**No essays.** Never write a sentence *about* the story: what the moment means, what the knight has learned about life, what kind of person this is. Show it with an action or a line of dialogue instead.

## Characters and dialogue

**Introduce people by what they look like, then let them speak.** They usually give their own name and title: *"I am Ederyn, King of the Marches, though you would not know it."* After that, the narration uses a short epithet: *the aged king*, *the rogue*, *the giantess*, *the fool*.

**Dialogue carries the exposition.** The character with the problem explains it, in their own voice, often at length. A 60-word speech in the middle of a response passage is normal. It is how the book delivers backstory.

**Give major speakers a verbal habit.** A king who punctuates everything with a snort, a fool who rhymes when nervous, a fay who never uses contractions, a mercenary who counts everything in coin. One habit per character, used lightly.

**Speech tags are plain:** says, asks, cries, calls, whispers, growls, sighs, snorts. Put the tag mid-sentence when the speaker needs a beat: *"Well," grunts the ferryman, "that is one way to cross a river."*

**The knight speaks too.** Resolution passages often begin with the knight saying the choice aloud. Outcome bodies often turn on what the knight says. Give the knight lines that are courteous, capable and occasionally wry.

**Forms of address.** "Good knight" is by far the most common (well over a hundred uses), then "worthy knight", "fair knight", "noble knight", "my lord", "my lady", "milady". Commoners say "good knight". Nobles say "sir knight" or use the name. Clergy say "my child" or "my son/daughter".

### The archaism budget

The published book uses about **one archaic word per thousand words**, mostly in dialogue. *Prithee* appears fewer than thirty times in the whole book.

| Use freely in dialogue | Use sparingly (a few per book section) | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| alas, lest, yonder, perchance, nay, milady | prithee, 'tis, thee, thou, thy, ye, methinks, hither, whither, betwixt, naught | forsooth, verily-as-filler, doth/hath in narration, pseudo-Middle-English spelling |

Narration stays modern. Old, royal and fay speakers get more archaism than young or common ones. That contrast is itself characterisation.

Also avoid obvious modern idiom ("okay", "deal with it", "stressful", "fine by me") and modern psychology words ("trauma", "closure", "boundaries").

## Humour and darkness

Both are part of the house style, often in the same encounter.

- **Humour** comes from character and situation, never from winking at the reader: a herald who has memorised the wrong lady's titles; a knight boasting of a battle to a stranger who, it turns out, fought on the other side; a king losing his crown at dice to a goose-girl.
- **Darkness** comes from consequence. A failed plea for calm can end in a killing. A kind act can bring a curse. Do not soften it with commentary. State what happened, give the knight a moment's reaction, and apply the status.

## Choices

Every response passage ends with its choices. Each begins "You may" and names a deed.

- **Start with a verb.** The verbs the published book reaches for most often: *help, try, ask, offer, attack, join, agree, attempt, confront, refuse, convince, enter, court, rob, follow, approach, argue, seek, fight, ignore, speak, assist, accept, steal, escort, challenge, seduce*.
- **Name the target and the aim**: *You may help King Ederyn win back his crown.* Not *You may help.*
- **Say theft, seduction and violence plainly.** Covetous, Licentious and Enraged knights *must* take any choice to *steal or rob*, *court or seduce*, or *attack, fight or joust*, so those verbs belong in the label (see rewards.md).
- **Never name a skill** in a choice. The deed implies the approach, and the skills are revealed at the resolution.
- **Make the fork a real one.** The strongest pairs are moral (help/rob, serve/oppose, heal/exploit) or methodical (by force / by wit). See plots.md for the full set.
- **Romantic choices** begin with `* ` (asterisk and space) and set `"romantic": true`. The book offers romance toward people of any sex and station, including unlikely ones such as a hag or a rival knight.
- **The walk-away** is phrased as moving on: *You may bid the pilgrims farewell and go your own way.*
- **Instead** is often used to mark the second option: *You may instead ask the old man about the chalice.*

## Resolution passages

A resolution passage has three parts.

1. **Commitment (0–3 sentences).** The knight carries out the choice, often with a line of speech that echoes it. *"No goose-girl in Britain can outwit a king and a knight together," you say, hauling Ederyn to his feet.* Sometimes a complication arrives here and sharpens the stakes.
2. **The skill offer.** In print this is one sentence: *You may use Cunning to win the crown back at dice, or you may use Diplomacy to persuade her to return it.* In `book.json`, each deed becomes a resolution option's `label` (a lowercase verb phrase), so the body does not need to repeat the sentence.
3. **The outcomes.** For each skill, a success body and a failure body.

### How to write an outcome pair

**Start both halves from the same moment.** In the published book, the success and failure text for a skill very often open with the same sentence, or the same line of the knight's dialogue, and diverge at one precise point: the dice land or are caught, the speech persuades or offends. Write the shared opening first, then the split.

**Success** is specific and has texture:
- something concrete changes: a gift, a door opened, a rival shamed, a villager saved;
- a character's reaction, usually spoken, often with humour or an unexpected note;
- a closing image or line. It is rarely pure triumph. There is usually a small cost, an irony, or a hint of a larger story.

**Failure** is a mishap, not a humiliation:
- a concrete thing goes wrong, and it is plausibly the knight's fault;
- the other character reacts, and they are allowed to be right;
- **close on the lesson or the consequence.** Many failures end with a short sentence in which the knight takes something away: *You resolve to be more careful with your tongue.* *You will not underestimate a miller again.* That sentence is what justifies the skill gained on failure. Say it plainly and briefly. Never write a moral about life in general.

Occasionally a failure does not end the scene. It hands the knight a second decision: *You may either go after him or let him go.* Use this sparingly (see passage-shapes.md).

## Result passages (no roll)

A result is reached directly from a choice. Typical uses: asking a character for their story, accepting a blessing, a quiet farewell. The shape:

- a character speaks, often telling their tale in one long, lively speech;
- a closing action or image;
- a small reward.

The "ask about his history" result is a signature of the book. The payoff is lore, told in character, with a Destiny and a skill or a rank.

## Walk-away passages

One to three sentences. Wry, not preachy. The knight moves on, and the reward is usually `Move 1 space`.

> *You decide the goose-girl's affairs are hers and the king's, and ride on while the two of them are still shouting. Behind you, a goose honks in what sounds like approval.* [Move 1 space]

## Anti-patterns

Most of these come from AI-generated books that drifted away from the original voice.

| Don't | Instead |
|---|---|
| Essayistic abstraction: *"He is in love, and love in a young knight is a kind of intoxication that makes everything seem possible."* | Show it: *"He has written her name on his shield in charcoal, and it has run in the rain."* |
| Aphoristic closings: *"Joy and skill are different things."* | End on an image, a line of dialogue, or the knight's concrete resolve. |
| Unnamed everyone: *a knight*, *a lady*, *the lord* | Name the people the knight speaks to. Minor characters get names too: Wenna, Fitzhugh, Brother Anselm. |
| Nothing happens: the whole passage is atmosphere | An event within two sentences. Atmosphere rides along with it. |
| Long chains of em-dashes and semicolons | Short sentences. Full stops. |
| Skills in choices: *You may use Piety to…* on a response | Deeds in choices. Skills appear only at resolution. |
| Heroic success with no texture | A reaction, a cost, a joke, or a thread. |
| Failure as pure punishment | A mishap, the other character's reaction, the lesson, the skill. |
| Naming Lancelot, Palomides, Gawain, Percival, Galahad, Tristan, Enid or Bradamante | They are the player characters. Leave them out. |
| Heavy archaism, *thee* and *thou* in narration | Modern narration. Spice in dialogue only. |

## A worked example

This is a complete encounter in the house style. It is original, with invented characters.

**Response: King + Lost**

> As you ford a shallow river at dusk, you hear a man cursing the water, the reeds and several saints in turn. On the far bank sits a stout fellow in a fine surcoat, soaked to the waist and bareheaded, wringing out one boot.
>
> "Good knight!" he calls. "You find me at a disadvantage. I am Ederyn, King of the Marches, though you would not know it, for I have no crown. I wagered it at dice last night against a goose-girl named Wenna, and the wretch threw three sixes." He glares downstream, where smoke rises from a cottage. "She will not give it back. She says it suits her geese better than it suits me, and my own steward agrees with her."
>
> You may help King Ederyn win back his crown.
> You may instead visit the goose-girl and bargain for the crown on your own account.

**Resolution: help the king**

> "No goose-girl in Britain can outwit a king and a knight together," you say, hauling Ederyn to his feet. He looks doubtful, but he squelches after you to the cottage, where Wenna sits on her doorstep with the crown on her knee and a goose asleep inside it.

*Cunning (4): win the crown back at dice.*

> **Success.** You sit down across from Wenna and propose a game. She shrugs, tips the goose out of the crown, and sets it between you. On your second throw you let the dice fall behind a stone where only you can see them. "Six and six," you announce. Wenna narrows her eyes but cannot prove a thing. She hands over the crown, and the king jams it onto his wet head and laughs until he coughs. "A knight of wit!" he cries. "Come to my hall at Candlemas and sit at my right hand."
> [Gain 2 Destiny | 1 Courtly Skill of your choice | 1 Rank of Villainy]
>
> **Failure.** You sit down across from Wenna and propose a game. She shrugs, tips the goose out of the crown, and sets it between you. On your second throw you try to let the dice fall behind a stone, but Wenna catches your wrist before they land. "My father was a soldier," she says, "and I have seen that trick in every alehouse from here to Chester." She keeps the crown, and the king does not speak to you all the way back to the river. You resolve to practise your sleights somewhere less observant.
> [Gain Cunning | Become Scorned]

*Diplomacy (3 + Location #): persuade Wenna to return it.*

> **Success.** "You won it fairly," you tell Wenna, "and no one here says otherwise. But a crown is a heavy thing to keep. Every lord's man in the Marches will come asking after it." Wenna glances at the road, then at the king. "I'd sooner have the three fat hens he keeps at his hall," she says. Ederyn agrees with suspicious speed. By nightfall the goose-girl has her hens, the king has his crown, and you have the thanks of both.
> [Gain Destiny = Location # | 1 Courtly Skill of your choice | 1 Rank of Renown]
>
> **Failure.** "You won it fairly," you tell Wenna, "but a crown is a heavy thing to keep." "Not so heavy as a goose," she says, and the goose in question wakes and bites you on the hand. By the time you have freed yourself, Wenna has gone inside and barred the door. "Kingship," sighs Ederyn, "is a lonely road." You bind your hand and ride on, a little wiser in the ways of hard bargains.
> [Gain Diplomacy]

What this shows:
- The response opens on the road, and the problem arrives in the second paragraph, in the king's own voice.
- The choices are a moral fork (help him or profit from him), with no skill named.
- Each outcome pair shares an opening and splits at one moment.
- Cheating succeeds but earns Villainy. The book lets the method colour the renown.
- Each failure closes on a concrete lesson, and gains the skill it tested.
- Humour and a named minor character carry the scene. There is no commentary.

## Final checklist for a passage

- [ ] Second person, present tense
- [ ] Something happens in the first two sentences
- [ ] Named people, a concrete detail each, and at least one line of dialogue (response passages)
- [ ] Choices start "You may", name a deed and a target, no skills
- [ ] Resolution opens with the knight committing, in action or speech
- [ ] Each outcome pair starts from the same moment and splits cleanly
- [ ] Failure ends on the lesson or the consequence, and gains a skill
- [ ] Success has texture: a reaction, a cost, a joke or a thread
- [ ] No essay sentences, no aphorisms, no modern idiom
- [ ] Archaism within budget
- [ ] No player-character names (see SKILL.md rule 9)
